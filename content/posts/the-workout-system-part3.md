This article is going to cover nutrition, cardio, stretching and supplements

Just think about what you've eaten over the past week. Consider the quality of your food, how processed it was, whether you calculated your calories and macros, whether you're getting enough vitamins and minerals, and what time of day you're eating.

Look, I'm not a dietitian or a doctor, and you don't have to be one to understand how nutrition works. The logic is simple: eat high-quality food, eat clean, eat enough and eat what your body needs. We'll get into the details after creating the foundation.

Before doing that, I strongly recommend checking your health with professionals, especially if you have any medical conditions. Some foods, supplements or dietary approaches might not be suitable for you. We're trying to improve our health here, not fuck it up.

## Total Calories and Macros

I hope your math is good, 'cause we're going to do some calculations.

By the way, this time the article is for everyone. The only thing I can't help you with is your taste in food. If you hate rice or oats, that's your problem, man.

First of all, we need these numbers:

1. **Current body weight:** Get on the scale in the morning, preferably after going to the bathroom and before eating anything.
2. **Height and age:** Pretty straightforward.
3. **Sex:** We'll need this for the calorie calculation.
4. **Body measurements:** Neck, shoulders, chest, arms, forearms, waist, hips, quads and calves. Yes, measure all of them and write them down. And don't suck in your stomach.
5. **Activity level:** How many times a week do you work out, at what intensity, and how active are you outside the gym?

Don't cheat. Be honest with yourself.

Now we need to calculate three things: BMI, daily calorie requirements and macronutrient targets.

### BMI

BMI stands for Body Mass Index, and we calculate it using this formula:

**BMI = Weight (kg) / Height (m)²**

For example, if you're 90 kg and 180 cm tall:

90 / (1.80 × 1.80) = **27.8 BMI**

According to standard BMI classifications, that's considered overweight. But there's a problem: BMI can't distinguish muscle from fat. A muscular guy and an overweight guy might have exactly the same BMI.

So, use BMI as a rough reference, not as the ultimate measurement of your health or physique. Your waist measurement, body composition and progress over time can tell you much more.

### Calories

Now, calories are a little more complicated, but don't worry, we'll handle them like professionals.

First, we need to calculate our Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), which estimates how many calories our body burns at rest.

We'll use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation:

**Male:** BMR = (10 × weight) + (6.25 × height) − (5 × age) + 5

**Female:** BMR = (10 × weight) + (6.25 × height) − (5 × age) − 161

Weight is in kilograms and height is in centimeters.

But that's just our resting energy expenditure. We also walk, work, train, move around and do hundreds of other things throughout the day. So we need to multiply our BMR by an estimated activity factor.

|Activity Level|Multiplier|
|---|---|
|Sedentary|1.2|
|Lightly active|1.375|
|Moderately active|1.55|
|Very active|1.725|

The result is our estimated Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE), basically how many calories we burn throughout an average day.

Now we have three options: losing fat, maintaining our current weight or gaining mass.

**Losing fat:** Eat fewer calories than you burn. A deficit of around 300–500 calories per day is a reasonable starting point for many people. If your maintenance is 2,900 calories, try approximately 2,400–2,600.

**Maintaining weight:** Eat approximately the same number of calories you burn. If your maintenance is 2,900, that's your starting target.

**Gaining mass:** Eat slightly more than you burn. A surplus of around 150–300 calories is a reasonable place to start if you want to gain muscle without accumulating unnecessary fat.

One important thing: these calculations are estimates. Track your average morning body weight for two or three weeks, then adjust your calories according to what actually happens. Your body doesn't give a shit about what an online calculator says.

### Macronutrients

Imagine you have 2,500 units of money to spend throughout the day. You would want to spend that money on high-quality things, right? Things that are actually useful and worth spending money on.

Calories work in a similar way. You've got a budget, and you should spend most of it on foods that provide your body with the energy and nutrients it needs.

We can split that budget into three main things: protein, carbohydrates and fats.

**Protein:** The building material for your muscles. It supports muscle repair, growth and maintenance. For people who train regularly, around 1.6–2.2 g/kg of body weight is a good target. Each gram of protein contains 4 calories.

**Carbohydrates:** My favorite one. Seriously, people underestimate how much carbohydrates can affect their training performance. They're an important source of energy for high-intensity exercise, and muscle glycogen plays a major role in demanding workouts. If you consistently eat too little for your training needs, your strength, endurance and recovery may suffer. Around 3–5 g/kg can work well for many recreational lifters, while athletes with greater training volumes may need considerably more. Each gram contains 4 calories.

**Fats:** Essential for normal cellular functions, hormone production and absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. Don't be afraid of fats, just get them from decent sources. Olive oil, nuts, seeds, eggs and fatty fish are good options. Around 0.6–1.0 g/kg is a useful starting range for many active people, provided total fat intake is adequate. Each gram contains 9 calories.

I personally prioritize carbohydrates after meeting my protein and fat requirements. I can hit those two pretty easily, but getting enough carbs to support my training takes a little more planning.

And don't forget micronutrients. Vitamins, minerals and fiber matter too. You can't just eat chicken and rice all day, take a multivitamin and call it perfect nutrition.

## Meal Program

I'll continue with an example so everything is easier to understand.

Our fictional athlete has these measurements:

- **Body weight:** 90 kg
- **Age:** 25
- **Height:** 180 cm
- **Sex:** Male
- **Activity:** Four high-intensity resistance-training sessions per week
- **Goal:** Maintain weight and improve training performance

First, we calculate his BMR:

(10 × 90) + (6.25 × 180) − (5 × 25) + 5 = **1,905 kcal**

Assuming a moderately active lifestyle:

1,905 × 1.55 = **2,953 kcal**

Let's use approximately 2,940 calories as our daily target. The activity multiplier is an estimate, so we'll adjust this later if his weight trend tells us it's wrong.

Now let's distribute those calories:

| Macro         | Amount | Calories  |
| ------------- | ------ | --------- |
| Protein       | 180 g  | 720       |
| Carbohydrates | 375 g  | 1,500     |
| Fat           | 80 g   | 720       |
| **Total**     |        | **2,940** |

There we go. Now we know how much we need to eat.

So, what do I do?

### Breakfast

Oats with high-protein milk and one scoop of protein powder.

Simple, easy to prepare and provides a good amount of protein, carbohydrates and fiber. Sometimes I add a banana or berries. Nothing fancy here.

### Main Meal (2–3 Hours Before Training)

My usual options are rice with chicken, rice with ground beef, pasta with chicken or pasta with ground beef.

Add vegetables. You need vitamins, minerals and fiber, not just protein and carbohydrates. I usually cook with olive oil or a little butter, depending on the meal.

Eating two or three hours before training works well for me because I have enough time to digest everything. You might prefer a different interval, so figure out what works for your stomach.

### Pre-Workout (30–60 Minutes Before Training)

One glass of warm water with some honey, a banana and black coffee. No milk, no sugar, just caffeine.

Honey and bananas provide easily digestible carbohydrates, while caffeine can improve alertness and exercise performance. Nothing magical about the honey being mixed with warm water, I just like it that way.

Keep in mind that caffeine isn't necessary, and too much can mess with your sleep or cause unpleasant side effects. Also, if you've already eaten enough and feel energetic, you don't need to force yourself to have an additional snack.

### Post-Workout

One scoop of protein powder, five eggs cooked with a little butter and some nuts.

That's one of my favorite combinations, but you can change the quantities and foods according to your needs. Five eggs aren't some scientifically perfect post-workout requirement.

Also, you don't need to drink your protein shake within 30 seconds of finishing your last set. Total daily protein intake matters more than obsessing over an extremely narrow anabolic window.

Don't forget to include carbohydrates in your remaining meals, especially after demanding workouts. Replenishing glycogen is part of recovery.

One last thing about this meal program: these are meal ideas, not an exact 2,940-calorie menu. You'll need to adjust the portions and add other meals until you reach your calorie and macro targets. Use a food scale if necessary, and remember that raw and cooked food weights are different.

## Cardio

You skip your cardio, right? If not, you're free to go. But if you do, come here, we have to talk.

Look, we're not playing games here. We're training our hearts and bodies to withstand different kinds of physical stress, and we can't achieve everything through building muscles and lifting weights.

Regular cardio improves cardiovascular fitness and can help reduce the risk of several chronic diseases. Being muscular is great, but being muscular and having decent cardiovascular endurance is much better.

Cardio is different from lifting, at least for me. It's a different kind of mental challenge. When you're running, your brain starts telling you to stop, slow down or go home. Learning to handle ordinary discomfort is part of the process, although there's obviously a difference between being uncomfortable and ignoring a genuine warning sign.

I personally don't do cardio on my upper or lower body days. Instead, I spread it across my days without lifting: one running session and one incline-walking session per week.

For running, I usually aim for around 30–45 minutes at a sustainable intensity, mostly Zone 2. That means breathing harder than normal but still being able to speak in sentences. I don't try to destroy myself every time I run. Harder intervals have their place, but not every cardio session needs to be a fucking survival test.

Walking is much easier to manage. I prefer incline treadmill walking for around 45–60 minutes, adjusting the speed and incline according to my conditioning.

These two sessions are my personal routine, not necessarily enough for everyone's cardiovascular health. General recommendations for adults are at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week, or an equivalent combination. Brisk walking throughout the week counts too.

And no, cardio isn't going to kill your muscle gains if you manage your training volume, nutrition and recovery properly.

Get your cardio done, my friend. Your heart deserves some attention too.

## Stretching

You know those guys who can't touch their toes, reach their backs or move their shoulders properly? Like a big fucking piece of wood.

We don't want that, right?

Building strength is important, but so is being able to move your body comfortably. Flexibility and mobility are different things, but both are worth working on.

You don't need hours of stretching. I personally like doing around five minutes in the morning and five minutes before bed, focusing mainly on my shoulders, back, hips, hamstrings and calves. That's my simple daily habit; if you have specific flexibility limitations, you might need more targeted work.

Hold your stretches gently and avoid forcing painful positions. Static stretches of around 20–30 seconds are a reasonable starting point.

Before lifting heavy weights, I prefer dynamic movements and proper warm-up sets instead of long static stretching sessions. Prolonged static stretching immediately before maximal strength or explosive exercises can temporarily reduce performance.

Stretching isn't some magical injury-prevention method, and it won't automatically fix your posture or eliminate muscle soreness. But it can improve range of motion, which is a good enough reason for me to keep doing it.

I don't want to become a 100 kg muscular guy who struggles to scratch his own back.

## Supplements

This is something you should be really careful about. I'm going to explain the supplements I use or consider useful, but that doesn't mean you should automatically start taking all of them.

I prefer making decisions based on evidence, my diet and medical advice when necessary. Supplements have different levels of evidence, and taking something you don't need isn't automatically beneficial.

### Creatine

Probably my favorite supplement, and one of the most researched in sports nutrition.

Creatine increases your muscles' phosphocreatine stores, helping regenerate ATP during short, high-intensity efforts. That can improve performance during repeated heavy sets or sprints and help you achieve greater strength and muscle gains over time.

**3–5 grams of creatine monohydrate daily** is enough for most healthy adults. You don't need expensive versions or complicated cycles. Just take it consistently.

Creatine can increase your body weight slightly because of additional water stored in your muscles. That's normal. Maintain proper hydration, but don't force yourself to drink ridiculous amounts of water just because you take creatine.

If you have kidney disease or another relevant medical condition, consult your doctor before using it.

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D plays an important role in calcium absorption, bone health, muscle function and normal immune function.

Your body produces it through sunlight exposure, and you can also get some from food. However, depending on your lifestyle, location and other factors, you might not get enough.

The important thing is that correcting a deficiency can be beneficial, but taking additional vitamin D when your levels are already adequate doesn't automatically improve testosterone, strength or muscle growth.

Excessive doses can also be harmful. Don't blindly take high doses because somebody on the internet told you to. Get professional advice when deficiency is suspected.

### Magnesium

Magnesium is involved in energy metabolism, muscle and nerve function, and hundreds of enzymatic reactions. Pretty important stuff.

There are multiple forms of magnesium, including oxide, citrate and glycinate. Magnesium citrate is generally absorbed better than oxide, while glycinate is often chosen for gastrointestinal tolerability.

Your diet matters more than the specific supplement form. Nuts, seeds, legumes and whole grains are good magnesium sources. If you need a supplement, check the amount of elemental magnesium and avoid unnecessarily high doses.

And don't expect magnesium to magically improve your sleep or recovery if you're already getting enough.

### Zinc

Zinc supports normal immune function, protein synthesis, wound healing and numerous other biological processes.

You'll find it in meat, seafood, dairy products, legumes and nuts.

There's a lot of bullshit online about zinc being some kind of natural testosterone booster. Zinc deficiency can negatively affect reproductive health, but taking extra zinc when you're not deficient isn't a reliable way to raise testosterone.

Excessive zinc intake can also interfere with copper absorption. So, get enough, but don't overdo it.

### Omega-3

Holy fat.

Omega-3 fatty acids, particularly EPA and DHA, are important components of cell membranes and support normal physiological functions. DHA is especially important in the brain and retina, while EPA and DHA can help reduce elevated triglycerides.

I prefer getting omega-3 from fatty fish such as salmon, sardines and mackerel. If you rarely eat fish, supplementation might be worth considering depending on your diet and health needs.

Just don't believe that fish oil automatically prevents every cardiovascular problem. The evidence for supplementing healthy people is more complicated than that.

Also, when buying fish oil, check the actual EPA and DHA amounts. A capsule labeled "1,000 mg fish oil" doesn't necessarily contain 1,000 mg of EPA and DHA.

### L-Citrulline

This one is a little different. I see it more as an optional pre-workout supplement.

L-citrulline is converted into arginine in the body, which contributes to nitric oxide production. Nitric oxide helps regulate blood-vessel dilation, which is one reason citrulline is popular among lifters chasing a better pump.

Some studies suggest modest improvements in resistance-training performance, but results are inconsistent. It's definitely not in the same evidence category as creatine.

Studies commonly use around 3–6 grams of L-citrulline or 6–8 grams of citrulline malate before exercise, although those two forms aren't directly interchangeable.

## Last Words

Don't smoke, don't abuse drugs, and avoid alcohol if you want to minimize its health risks. None of them is worth sacrificing your health for.

Eat high-quality food. And look, I'm saying high-quality food, I'm not telling you to become obsessed with your diet. Eat pizza or burgers sometimes, enjoy your food and spend time with your friends. Just don't make shitty fast food the foundation of your nutrition.

Your body, your organs, your health, these things matter more than most people realize. If you seriously damage your health, you might not always be able to get it back.

Train hard, eat well, do your cardio and take care of your body.

Be healthy.
