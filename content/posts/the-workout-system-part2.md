Legs. Absolute units of mass. Some of the biggest and strongest muscle groups in your body. Underestimated health boosters, aesthetically pleasing columns holding up this bloody Colosseum of a physique.

You cannot skip leg day. There is not a single chance of even thinking about skipping leg day (of course, unless you have a legitimate medical reason, like having knee surgery tomorrow).

There are decades of research showing why leg muscles are so important for your general health. They play a major role in blood sugar regulation, metabolic health, bone density, and maintaining physical function as you age. Your calves even act as a "second heart," helping pump blood from your lower legs back toward your actual heart.

And when it comes to aging, maintaining muscle mass and strength is one of the best things you can do for yourself. No shitty expensive cream is going to replace what strong muscles do for your body.

So, what are we going to do?

**HIT YOUR LEGS WITH SOME GODDAMN FUCKING HEAVY WEIGHTS, MY DEAR FRIEND.**

First of all, before I start explaining the lower-body workout, I have to tell you that **this is not for beginners.** You can read it, but don't try to execute the entire program on your first leg day. I take no responsibility if you end up carrying your knees around in your hands.

We need to understand how your lower body works.

There are three main joints we're going to focus on: **hips, knees, and ankles.**

Your hips are basically the connection between your upper and lower body. They play a massive role in transferring force, stabilizing your pelvis, and controlling movement. Problems with hip strength, mobility, or control can affect how other parts of your body move and handle loads.

Your knees are where two of the biggest muscle groups in your legs do much of their work: the **quadriceps and hamstrings**. The quadriceps are mainly responsible for extending your knees, while the hamstrings help flex your knees and extend your hips. And of course, neither is a single muscle. There are multiple muscles involved, and if we start talking about every single one of them, this article will turn into an anatomy lesson.

Your ankles are the joints connecting your legs to your feet. They handle force transfer, balance, and a huge amount of the work involved in walking, running, jumping, and squatting.

Why are we talking about this? Just give me the workout!

No.

First, make sure you can actually control your body. Work on your mobility, stability, and any limitations that affect your movements. You don't need some magical perfect posture, but you do need enough control to perform the exercises properly.

If you have persistent pain or significant movement limitations, get professional help. Make sure you can perform a bodyweight squat through a comfortable range of motion without pain, losing balance, or having your heels uncontrollably lift off the ground.

Seriously, make sure you can handle your own body weight before putting a heavy-ass barbell on your back.

Now, we have two main exercises we're going to focus on: **squat variations (especially the back squat) and the Romanian deadlift (RDL).**

The squat is my lifelong best friend. I just love how brutally demanding it is. Nothing quite compares to finishing a heavy set of squats and suddenly forgetting every stupid little problem you had throughout the day, lol.

But remember, muscular effort and burning are one thing. Sharp joint pain is another. Don't confuse them.

The RDL is one of our main exercises for the posterior chain. Yes, it's a deadlift variation, but unlike the conventional deadlift, we're emphasizing the hip hinge, with most of the dynamic work coming from the glutes and hamstrings. Your spinal erectors are involved too, mainly to keep your back stable throughout the movement.

Everything else, like abductors, adductors, leg extensions, and leg curls, is the seasoning that makes our beef even more delicious.

We're going to split the lower body into two variations, just like the upper body: **Lower A and Lower B.**

Lower A focuses on heavy compound movements, strength, and muscle growth. Lower B focuses on unilateral strength, balance, stability, and being able to do cool shit with your legs.

Here we go.

## Lower A: Heavy Day

- **Back squat:** 2×6–8, 1 RIR. I personally prefer heel-elevated back squats because I want to emphasize my quadriceps. You can choose another squat variation if it fits your body and goals better. Just make sure you can load it heavily while maintaining control. I also do one optional back-off set at around 50% of my 1RM with a really, really slow eccentric and concentric: 3 seconds down, hold for 2 seconds at the bottom, and 3 seconds up. Why? Man, I really don't know. I just love the burning sensation. There's nothing magical about this tempo, so skip it if you don't want to do it.
    
- **Leg press:** 2×6–8, 1 RIR. Nothing fancy. Just make sure your quadriceps are doing the work instead of turning the exercise into a competition to see how much your lower back can suffer. Keep your pelvis and lower back stable against the pad, and don't force a range of motion that makes them roll off the seat. People somehow manage to turn a leg press into a lower-back exercise, which is honestly impressive in the worst possible way.
    
- **RDL:** 2×6–8, 1 RIR, plus 1×6–8, 2 RIR. I prefer doing these without straps because I want my grip and forearms to work too. But if your grip gives out before your hamstrings and glutes, use straps. We're training legs here, not trying to win a grip-strength competition.
    
- **Weighted back extension:** 2×6–8, 1–2 RIR. Set up the machine and perform the movement in a way that emphasizes hip extension, targeting your glutes and hamstrings. Your spinal erectors will still work, of course, but don't turn this into uncontrolled lower-back hyperextension. And if your lower back is already completely cooked from the RDLs, don't force the weight.
    
- **Leg extension:** 2×8–10, 1 RIR. Let those quadriceps burn in hell. Control the weight, use a comfortable range of motion, and don't start kicking the machine like it owes you money.
    
- **Leg curl (seated or lying):** 2×8–10, 1 RIR. Make sure you're actually training your hamstrings instead of throwing the weight around. Both variations work, so choose whichever feels better and allows you to progress consistently.
    
- **Abductor and adductor:** 2×10–12 each, 1 RIR. Nothing will kill you, but these two exercises might make sitting down feel like a complicated engineering problem.

The volume is high, and the intensity of effort is high too.

Your legs can probably handle a lot, but that doesn't mean recovery is unlimited. If your performance starts dropping, you're constantly exhausted, or you're still destroyed by the next session, reduce some accessory volume instead of just pushing harder.

And remember, **the goal is to get stronger, not to collect as much muscle soreness as humanly possible.**

## Lower B: Cool Shit

Yeah, I know. Lifting heavy weights feels amazing, and it makes your legs stronger and bigger.

But what about balancing your body on one leg? What about differences in strength and control between your left and right legs? What about being able to control your body in different positions instead of only moving a barbell up and down?

That's what Lower B is for.

- **Pistol squat (BOSU optional):** 2×6–8 per leg, 1–2 RIR on a stable surface. I honestly don't know how I ended up doing pistol squats on a BOSU ball, but I enjoy them, and they've helped me challenge my balance and control. Just don't confuse extra instability with extra strength gains. Do your main strength work on stable ground, and treat BOSU pistol squats as an optional, unloaded balance challenge once you're already comfortable with regular pistols. If a pistol squat is too much, try an assisted pistol squat. If that's still too much, do Bulgarian split squats. And if those are too difficult too, you probably need to work on your unilateral strength, balance, or mobility. Nothing wrong with that. Just build it up.
    
- **Single-leg RDL:** 2×6–8 per leg, 1 RIR. Use dumbbells, please. Control your hips, keep your balance, and don't turn every repetition into some weird spinning movement. The goal is to train your posterior chain while challenging single-leg stability, not to perform an interpretive dance.
    
- **Leg extension:** 3×10–12, 1–2 RIR. Pistol squats and other single-leg squat variations already train your quadriceps, but their balance and coordination demands can sometimes limit how hard you can actually load those muscles. So we're adding leg extensions to make sure the quads get enough direct work.
    
- **Leg curl (seated or lying):** 3×10–12, 1–2 RIR. Same idea as the leg extension. Single-leg RDLs train the hamstrings mainly through hip extension, while leg curls train them through knee flexion. We want both movement patterns covered.
    
- **Abductor and adductor:** 2×10–12 each, 1–2 RIR. Just for the love of the game.
    
- **Standing calf raise:** 2×10–15, 1–2 RIR. Yes, I know I called your calves your second heart and then almost forgot to train them. Control the stretch at the bottom, push through a comfortable range of motion, and don't bounce your way through the set.


That's it.

Lower A is pretty straightforward. It's a classic heavy lower-body workout, although the combination of exercises and effort can be extremely demanding.

Lower B is a little different. It challenges your unilateral strength, coordination, and balance in ways that heavy bilateral lifting doesn't fully cover. It doesn't magically activate muscles that have never worked before, but it can expose weaknesses and limitations that you might not notice during regular squats and deadlifts.

Please, warm up properly before leg day. Get your body moving, do some lighter ramp-up sets before your heavy lifts, and make sure you're ready for the weights you're about to use.

If you enjoy stretching after a heavy workout, go ahead. Just don't expect it to magically prevent soreness. Proper recovery, sleep, nutrition, and sensible training volume matter a lot more.

**Burn your legs like they're in hell.**

Just make sure they're actually getting stronger while you're doing it.
